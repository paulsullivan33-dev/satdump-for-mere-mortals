# What SatDump Is

Satellites are talking over your house right now. Weather satellites in polar orbit cross the sky every hour or so, broadcasting pictures of the Earth as they go, in the open, unencrypted, free for anyone with a receiver to pick up. **SatDump** is the program that turns those broadcasts into something a person can look at: it takes a **recording of radio signals** and produces **pictures and data files** — a cloud photograph taken from orbit, a temperature map, a table of numbers.

One program does this for a surprising share of everything up there. The copy this guide's lab installed ships **184 pipelines** — a pipeline is a complete recipe for one kind of transmission — covering the American NOAA weather satellites, Russia's Meteor-M series, the geostationary GOES and Himawari satellites that stare at one hemisphere all day, deep-space probes, cubesats built by universities, and more. The same program, the same idea, each time: recording in, products out.

That idea — **the recording is the starting point** — is what makes SatDump different from older satellite software, and it is worth pausing on. Traditionally, receiving a satellite was a live event: the satellite rose, you tuned the radio, the software decoded in real time, and if your antenna or your settings were wrong, the pass was gone. SatDump still offers that live mode, but its centre of gravity is the file. If you have a recording, you can decode it today, next year, on a different computer, with different settings, as many times as you like. People share recordings the way they share photographs. This guide's lab decoded a pass that was recorded in **December 2018** — the satellite, the weather, and the recording all long past — and got the same picture the receiver got, plus a few products the receiver never asked for.

A word about what SatDump is *not*. It is not a radio. It does not receive anything by itself; a separate program (or SatDump's own live mode, with a supported **software-defined radio**) does the listening, and chapter 12 talks about the neighbours that record. It is also not a picture viewer with delusions of grandeur: between the recording and the picture sits real signal processing — demodulating the radio waveform, synchronising to the data stream, decoding error-corrected frames — and when any of that fails, SatDump fails in ways this guide teaches you to read rather than hide.

Finally, an honest framing that the whole guide keeps: **nothing here is secret, and nothing here is a trick.** These satellites broadcast for the public, on published frequencies, precisely so that schools, amateurs and weather services can use them. Receiving them is a well-established hobby with decades of history. The ethics rule for this guide is the same one the hobby runs on: receive what is broadcast openly, never transmit on satellite frequencies without a licence, and never present a decoded picture as something it is not — which is why this guide is careful, everywhere, to say exactly where its one decoded picture came from.

# Beyond NOAA — Other Satellites

APT is the gentle end of the pool. This chapter walks the rest of the catalogue a reader is likely to attempt, with an honest account of what each step up costs. The pipelines named are all in the installed program (chapter 5); the reception facts are the project's and the hobby's common knowledge, and — except for APT — the lab did not decode these, so this chapter is labelled accordingly: it is a map, not a lab report.

**Meteor-M LRPT — the natural second satellite.** Russia's Meteor-M2 series sends digital pictures on 137.1 MHz, near APT's frequencies, so the same class of antenna serves. The difference is the signal's character: APT is analogue and degrades gracefully — a weak pass is a noisy picture — while LRPT is digital with error correction, so a pass is either decoded or full of holes where the correction gave up. The reward is three instrument channels combined into true-colour images considerably sharper than APT's. The pipelines are `meteor_m2_lrpt` and its siblings for the newer satellites in the series. Input, as with APT, can be a baseband recording from your receiver software.

**HRPT — the same satellites, the hard way.** NOAA and Meteor both carry a high-rate downlink near 1.7 GHz carrying all their instrument channels at full resolution. The pictures are the ones APT crops: five channels, every composite chapter 7 said was impossible suddenly available. The cost is hardware: at 1.7 GHz you need a dish, a low-noise amplifier, and tracking that follows the satellite across the sky — a fixed antenna will not do. Pipelines: `noaa_hrpt`, `meteor_hrpt`, and the Metop series' equivalents in `MetOp.json`.

**Geostationary — GOES and friends.** A different bargain entirely. The satellite never moves in the sky, so a fixed dish works and there is no "pass" to wait for — instead, images of the whole Earth disc arrive on a timetable, day and night. The American GOES pipelines (`goes_hrit`, `goes_grb`, and ten more for its various relays) cover the Americas; Himawari covers East Asia and the Pacific; Meteosat covers Europe and Africa. Frequencies are higher, dishes are dishes, and the downlink formats are more demanding of the receiver — but the *processing* is the same SatDump shape: recording (or live stream) in, pipeline, folder of products out.

**The long tail.** Past the weather fleet, the catalogue's 184 pipelines include ocean-colour and land-imaging missions whose data is scientific rather than pictorial, cubesats sending telemetry and small images, and one-off targets like DSCOVR's Earth portraits from a million miles out. These are real but specialised — most need their own antennas, frequencies and patience, and several "pipelines" exist so that one research group could process one satellite's dumps. Browse the catalogue the way the lab counted it, by opening the pipeline files themselves; the frequency lists inside each one are the truest guide to what a reception would actually require.
